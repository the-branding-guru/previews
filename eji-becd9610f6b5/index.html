Everything Just Is. Movement One. Just Is.

Chapter One. There Is No Verdict.

There's no such thing as wins and losses. That's people that see black and white, zeros and ones. I see every shade in between.

Something happens. Then you decide what it means.

Those are two different events. They feel like one, because the second one arrives so fast you never see the gap. The car cuts you off and you are already angry. The text goes unanswered and you already know what it means. The deal falls through and it is already a failure, already a sign, already a story about you. It feels like the world handed you the ruling along with the facts.

It didn't. The world hands you the facts. The ruling is yours. You add it. And here is the thing nobody tells you: the adding is the only part that costs anything.

The car cut you off. That's it. That's the whole event. It took a second and a half and it is over. The anger you carried into the office, the replay at lunch, the version you told at dinner, the tightness still in your shoulders at ten o'clock — none of that was the car. All of that was the verdict. The event was free. The verdict you paid for all day.

I want you to sit with that for a minute, because the whole book is inside it.

The gap.

Let me tell you how I see things, and then you can decide whether it's useful to you.

I don't see wins and losses. I never have. When I say that to people, they think I'm being humble, or that I'm doing some kind of positive-thinking trick on myself. I'm not. I mean it literally. When I look at a situation, I don't see a scoreboard. I see a situation. It has a shape, it has details, it has a hundred shades in it, and none of those shades is called "good" or "bad." Those two words are not in the picture. They are something people bring to the picture, and then they walk around thinking they saw it there.

Black and white. Zeros and ones. That is how a machine sees. A machine has to sort everything into two bins because it can't hold a shade. It can only hold on or off. And a lot of people have trained themselves to see the same way, because two bins feel safer than a hundred shades. If it's a win, I can relax. If it's a loss, I know what to do — feel bad. At least the feeling is familiar.

But you are not a machine, and reality is not made of two bins. Reality is every shade in between, all at once, all the time. When you force it into two bins you don't get a clearer picture. You get a smaller one. You lose everything that didn't fit.

So here is the first move, and it is the only move in this chapter. Find the gap. Between the thing that happened and the thing you decided it meant, there is a space. It is small. It is real. Everything in this book lives in that space.

What you actually suffered over.

Think about the worst week you had this year. Don't pick a tragedy; pick an ordinary bad week. The kind where nothing went right and by Thursday you were done.

Now go back through it honestly and separate the events from the verdicts.

The events were things like: a client changed their mind. A person you counted on didn't show. You said something in a meeting that came out wrong. Money you expected didn't come. Somebody was rude to you for no reason.

Each of those took a few minutes, or a few seconds. Add them all up and the events themselves might fill an hour of a hundred-and-sixty-eight-hour week.

Now look at what filled the rest. The replaying. The what-does-this-say-about-me. The what-should-I-have-done. The they-shouldn't-have. The this-always-happens. The it's-going-to-be-like-this-forever. The tightness. The short sleep. The snapping at someone who had nothing to do with it.

All of that was the second thing. The verdict. You suffered over everything twice, and the second time was the one that hurt.

I am not saying the events were nothing. Some events are heavy. Some cost real money and real time and real trust. But look at the proportion. An hour of events, a week of verdicts. That ratio is the same for almost everybody I've ever talked to, and most people have never once measured it. They think the week was bad. The week was fine. The rulings were bad.

It's not positive thinking.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying, because there is a cheap version of this idea and I don't want you to confuse it with the real one.

I am not saying everything is good. I am not saying look on the bright side. I am not saying find the silver lining, or it happened for a reason, or the universe has a plan. Every one of those is a verdict. It's just a nicer-sounding verdict. You still added it. You still paid for it. You just paid for a pleasant story instead of a painful one, and pleasant stories collapse the second reality stops cooperating.

What I am saying is smaller than that and much harder. I am saying: there is no verdict. Not a good one. Not a bad one. The event happened. It just is.

That sounds cold when you first hear it. It isn't. It is the warmest thing you can do for yourself, because it gives you back everything you were spending on the ruling. You get the energy back. You get the hours back. You get the shoulders back. And you get something else that takes longer to notice: you get to see what actually happened, in every shade, instead of the two-bin cartoon of it.

A person who sees every shade has more options than a person who sees two. That's not philosophy. That's just math.

Where the verdict comes from.

You might be wondering why we do this, if it costs so much. Fair question.

The verdict feels like protection. If I decide right away that this was bad, I feel like I've done something about it. I've filed it. I've assigned blame. I've learned my lesson. The ruling gives the mind a place to put the thing, and the mind loves having a place to put things.

The verdict also feels like identity. I'm the kind of person who wins. I'm the kind of person things go wrong for. I'm unlucky. I'm sharp. Every ruling is a small vote for a story about who you are, and the stories are comfortable even when they're miserable, because at least you know the ending.

And the verdict is a habit. You started doing it before you can remember. Somebody older than you was doing it out loud, and you learned it the way you learned to talk. Good boy, bad boy. Good day, bad day. Good job, bad job. By the time you were ten you were a full-time judge with no salary and no days off.

None of that makes the verdict true. It just makes it familiar. And familiar is the most expensive thing in your life, because you never think to check the price.

What I actually do.

When something happens now, I try to let it be what it is for a moment before I let it be anything else.

That's the whole technique. There is no trick. The car cuts me off, and instead of the anger arriving in the same instant, there's a half-second where it's just a car and a lane and a guy who probably wasn't thinking about me at all. Most of the time that half-second is enough. The verdict doesn't form, because it turns out it never had anything to stand on. It was just going to be a reflex, and a reflex you catch is a reflex that fades.

Sometimes the half-second isn't enough and the verdict forms anyway. That's fine. I'm not perfect at this and I'm not trying to be. What I do then is notice that I've added something, and I look at what I added. Usually it's a "should." He shouldn't have done that. This shouldn't be happening. I should have known better. The should is the tell. Any time you hear a should, you are looking at a verdict, not an event.

I'll give the should its own chapter, because it deserves one. For now, just learn to hear it.

Every shade.

I said I see every shade in between, and I want to tell you what that actually looks like, because it isn't mystical.

A deal falls through. Two-bin thinking says: loss. Every-shade thinking says: the deal fell through, and inside that there is a client who is under pressure I didn't know about, a number I quoted that I can now see was ambitious, a conversation I had on Tuesday that I could have had on Monday, a relationship that is still intact, a lesson about timing I'll have for the rest of my life, a week that just opened up on my calendar, and a version of this deal that comes back in six months because I handled the ending well. That is one event. That is eight shades of it, off the top of my head, and there are more.

Which person is better off? The one who filed it as a loss and felt bad for a week, or the one who saw eight things and used six of them?

That's not a rhetorical question. It's the whole reason I wrote this. The person who sees the shades has more to work with. Always. And the only thing standing between you and the shades is the verdict, which you are adding, which you can stop adding, which costs nothing to stop.

There is no verdict. There is what happened, and there is what you do next. Everything else is a tax you're paying to a court that doesn't exist.

Practice. For one day, from the moment you wake up, notice every "should" and "shouldn't" as it forms. In your head, out loud, about yourself, about other people, about the weather. Don't argue with them. Don't fix them. Just count them. Keep the number. Do nothing else with it today. Tomorrow we'll talk about what it cost you.

Chapter Two. Judgement Is a Tax.

Judgement is overrated and a waste of energy.

How many did you count?

If you did yesterday's practice honestly, you have a number, and it is bigger than you expected. Most people who do this for the first time land somewhere between forty and a hundred and they stop counting by lunch, because it gets embarrassing. He should have called. She shouldn't wear that. I should be further along by now. The line shouldn't be this long. They should fix this road. I shouldn't have eaten that. This shouldn't take so long. He should know better.

Every one of those is a verdict. And every verdict is paid for.

That's the chapter. Judgement is a tax. I want to show you the bill.

One account.

Here is something I believe about people that I have never seen proven wrong.

You have one account. Not one for work, one for family, one for your health, one for your dreams. One. Everything you do in a day gets paid out of it. The energy you use to build something, the energy you use to love somebody, the energy you use to sit with a problem until it opens, the energy you use to get off the couch — one account. When it's empty, it's empty for everything at once. You don't get to be out of energy for your job and full of it for your kids. You are just out.

Now look at where the withdrawals go.

Every should you counted yesterday was a withdrawal. Small ones, most of them. A few seconds of heat, a small clench, a little story. But you counted sixty of them, and sixty small withdrawals a day is a real number. And that's the ones you caught. The ones that ran under the surface, the ones that had been running since you were a kid, the background hum of things-shouldn't-be-like-this — those you didn't count, and those are the bulk of the bill.

Then there are the big ones. The grudge you've been holding for three years is a standing order out of that account. It pays every day whether you think about it or not. The verdict you passed on yourself in your twenties, the one that says you're the kind of person who blows it — that's a mortgage. You've been paying it so long you think it's rent.

So when people tell me they're tired, or stuck, or they can't find the energy to start the thing they keep saying they'll start, I don't ask about their sleep. I ask about their verdicts. Because I know where the money went.

What it's buying you.

Fair question: if judgement costs this much, it must be buying something. What?

Let's be honest about it. It buys a feeling of being right. It buys a feeling of being on top of things, of having sorted the world. It buys a little hit of superiority when the verdict is about someone else and a little hit of familiarity when it's about you. It buys the sense that you have done something, that you have responded.

And every one of those is fake. Not bad. Fake. The feeling of being right doesn't make you right. The feeling of having sorted the world doesn't sort it. The hit of superiority is gone in four seconds and you're back where you were, minus the energy. And the sense that you've responded is the most expensive lie of all, because the verdict feels like action, so you don't take any.

You judged the guy who cut you off. Did the traffic get better? You judged your brother for the thing he said at dinner. Did your brother change? You judged yourself for the year you lost. Did you get it back?

No. You paid, and nothing shipped. That is the definition of a tax.

The word.

I want to give you the mechanism, because once you see it you can't stop seeing it, and that's the point.

There's a whole branch of psychology that studies how people's thinking goes wrong in predictable ways, and one of the oldest patterns on the list is the "should statement." The people who mapped this out — a doctor named Aaron Beck, a psychologist named Albert Ellis — found that when a person holds a rigid demand about how things must be, they suffer every time reality doesn't match. Not sometimes. Every time. Because reality has no obligation to match, and it usually doesn't. Ellis had a name for it that I've always loved. He called it musterbation. Things must be this way. People must treat me this way. Life must be fair. You can see why he picked the word.

The fix they found is so simple that people skip past it, so I'm going to say it slowly.

You change the demand into a preference.

"People should be kind" becomes "I would prefer people to be kind." That's it. That's the whole rewrite. And the reason it works isn't that it's softer. It's that the second sentence is true and the first one isn't. People are not obligated to be kind. That is simply a fact about the world. When you say "should," you're arguing with the fact. When you say "I would prefer," you're standing in the fact and choosing what you want from inside it.

Look at what happens to the energy.

"He should have called." Now you're waiting for a world in which he called, and that world doesn't exist, and every minute you wait is a minute you're paying for. "I would have preferred he called." Now you're in this world, the one where he didn't, and you have all your energy available to decide what to do about it. Call him. Don't. Let it go. Bring it up next time. Whatever. You're free to move, because you're not holding the door open for a version of events that already closed.

That's not weakness. It's not letting people off the hook. It is the difference between arguing with the weather and putting on a coat.

A day without it.

Let me show you the other side of the ledger, because the bill is only half the picture.

Try to imagine one full day where you pass no verdicts. Not one. You wake up and the alarm just is. The coffee is whatever the coffee is. The traffic just is — it's cars, going where cars go. The email that would normally make your jaw tighten arrives and it's information. Someone is short with you and that's their morning, not your problem. You make a mistake at eleven and it's a mistake, and you fix it, and it's fixed, and there is no second event where you decide what kind of person makes mistakes like that.

What does that day feel like from the inside?

I can tell you, because I've had a lot of them now, and the first thing you notice is not peace. Peace comes later. The first thing you notice is that you have a lot of extra time. Not on the clock — the clock is the same. In your head. The replays are gone. The rehearsals are gone. The arguments you were having with people who aren't in the room are gone. You keep reaching for the thing you usually chew on and it isn't there, and for a while that's almost uncomfortable, like a tongue looking for a tooth.

And then you notice what you can do with the space.

You can hear what the person in front of you is actually saying, because you're not composing the ruling while they talk. You can see the shape of a problem, because you're not busy deciding whose fault it is. You can start the thing. You can finish the thing. You can be in the room with your kid instead of in your head with your day. The energy that was going to the court comes back, and it turns out it was a lot of energy, and it turns out you had plans for it all along.

That's the return. Not a good feeling. A refund.

It's not about being soft.

People hear all this and think I'm telling them to stop having standards. I'm not. Read The Supreme Man if you want to know how I feel about standards. That book is the other hand and it's cold on purpose.

This is not about lowering the bar. It's about where you spend your energy in relation to the bar.

A person with high standards and no verdicts is the most effective person in any room. They know exactly what they want. They see exactly what's happening, in every shade. And they spend zero energy being outraged that the two don't match, which leaves them all their energy for closing the gap. That's the leader I'm going to talk about in a few chapters. The boss has the same standards and spends all day judging everybody for missing them, and wonders why the room is tired.

Standards say: this is what I'm building toward. Verdicts say: this is what should have already happened and didn't, and somebody has to pay. One of those moves the work. One of those moves nothing and costs everything.

The rewrite.

Yesterday you counted. Today you rewrite.

Take three of the shoulds from your count. Not the biggest ones — you're not ready for the mortgage yet. Three ordinary ones. Write each one down exactly the way it sounded in your head. Then, underneath, write the same thought as a preference. Not a nicer version. A true version.

"He should have called." → "I would have preferred he called."

"I should be further along by now." → "I would prefer to be further along, and I'm here."

"This line shouldn't be this long." → "I'd prefer a shorter line. It's this long."

Read them back. Notice that nothing about the world changed. He still didn't call. You're still where you are. The line is still long. And notice that something about you changed anyway. The argument stopped. The account stopped paying out. You're standing in what is, with your hands free.

That's what I mean when I say everything just is. Not that it's fine. That it's here, and the only thing you were adding was the fight.

Practice. Take three of yesterday's "shoulds." Write each one exactly as it sounded. Under each one, write it again as "I would prefer." Read both versions out loud. Keep the paper. You'll want it later, when you're doing this with the big ones.

Chapter Three. L's and W's.

I always take an L with a smile because L's are lessons, and W's are wisdom, not wins. You can't gain any wisdom without a lesson, and your biggest advancements in wisdom come from your hardest lessons. So I take all lessons on, with open arms.

You've done two chapters on the present. Now we go back.

Everything you've read so far was about the verdict you pass on what's happening today. But the biggest verdicts you carry aren't about today. They're about things that happened years ago, rulings you handed down so long ago you've forgotten they were rulings. You think they're facts. That was the year I blew it. That was the relationship that broke me. That was the deal that should have made me and didn't. Those aren't memories. They're sentences, and you've been serving them.

This chapter is where we open the file.

The alphabet.

When I was younger, I heard people talk about taking L's, and I understood it the way everybody does. An L is a loss. You lost. You took one on the chin. Somebody says "took an L" and the whole table winces.

At some point I stopped seeing it that way, and I don't think it was a decision so much as something I noticed. Every L I had ever taken had taught me something. Not in a greeting-card way. In a specific, measurable, I-do-this-differently-now way. The deal that fell apart taught me how to read a room before I walked into it. The person who burned me taught me what the early signs look like, and I have never missed them since. The year everything went wrong taught me what I actually needed versus what I'd been told I needed, and it turned out to be a much shorter list.

So I started calling them what they were. L's are lessons. That's not a slogan. That's the accurate label. What else would you call an event that changes how you operate for the rest of your life?

And if L's are lessons, then W's can't be wins, because a win doesn't teach you anything. A win confirms what you already thought. It feels great and it leaves you exactly as wise as you were the day before. So W's are wisdom. Wisdom is what you have after the lesson lands. It's the only thing you actually get to keep.

Lessons and wisdom. That's the whole alphabet. Wins and losses were never real. They were the two-bin version of the same thing, and you already know what I think about two bins.

The tuition.

Here's the part people don't want to hear.

You cannot get wisdom without a lesson. There's no other door. Nobody has ever become wise by having things go their way. Read any biography you want. The person you admire, the one who seems to see around corners, the one whose judgement you'd trust with your life — go look at what they went through. Every single one of them paid. Every single one of them has a year, or a decade, or a person, that they would not wish on anybody, and every single one of them will tell you that's where the sight came from.

And here's the sharper version of that: your biggest advancements in wisdom come from your hardest lessons. Not your medium ones. Not the ones you shrugged off. The ones that flattened you. The ones you still don't like to talk about. The correlation isn't loose. It's tight. The harder it hit, the more it taught, because the harder it hit, the less of your old picture survived, and the less of your old picture survived, the more room there was for a true one.

So when I say I take an L with a smile, I'm not being brave and I'm not being a fool. I'm doing the arithmetic. Something just happened that I would not have chosen. That means something is about to be taught that I could not have learned any other way. And the size of the hit tells me the size of the lesson. Of course I smile. I just got handed something I can't buy.

Open arms. Not because it doesn't hurt. Because I know what's in the box.

The file.

Now go back to yours.

I want you to think about the hardest thing that ever happened to you that you have never called a lesson. You know the one. It's the one you still call a loss, or a mistake, or a waste, or the thing that set you back. It might be a person. It might be money. It might be a version of yourself you're still embarrassed by. It's the file you don't open, or the one you open too often, and either way it's still labeled wrong.

Ask yourself one question about it, and answer honestly, and take your time.

What do I know now that I could not have known without it?

Not what you wish you'd known before. What you know now, because of it. Be specific. Not "I learned a lot." What, exactly, do you see now that you couldn't see then? What do you do differently? What do you refuse now that you used to accept? What do you catch early that used to catch you?

Most people, when they actually sit with this, come up with more than one thing. And then something shifts that I can't fully describe, except to say that the file gets relabeled while you're looking at it. It doesn't stop being painful. It stops being a loss. It becomes the most expensive class you ever took, and the class is over, and you passed, and you kept everything you learned.

That's the first time the reader gets their own story back. Not a nicer version of it. The true one.

Why this isn't a trick.

I want to head off something, because I know how this can sound.

This is not "everything happens for a reason." I don't believe things happen for a reason, and if I did I wouldn't have written chapter one. Things happen. That's it. There is no author handing you lessons on purpose. Nobody arranged your worst year so you'd grow.

The lesson isn't in the event. The lesson is in what you do with it. The same thing can happen to two people and one of them walks away with wisdom and the other walks away with a grudge, and the difference isn't the event. It's the label. The one who called it a lesson went looking for what it taught and found it. The one who called it a loss went looking for who to blame and found that instead. Both of them found what they were looking for. Only one of them got to keep something.

So this isn't fate. It's a decision you make after the fact, and it's the most profitable decision available to you, and it's free.

The smile.

People ask me about the smile. They think it's for show, or they think it means I don't feel things.

The smile is the moment the verdict doesn't form.

Something happens. The old reflex wants to file it: loss. The smile is what it feels like when you don't. When you stand in the gap from chapter one, and instead of the ruling, you have a different thought, which is: okay. What's in here. Show me. That thought has a shape, and the shape is a smile, and it isn't fake. It's the face of somebody who knows a delivery just arrived.

You won't manage it every time. I don't. Some things hit hard enough that the smile comes a week later, or a year later. That's fine. Late is fine. The lesson doesn't expire. It waits in the file until you're ready to open it, and the day you open it and relabel it, you get the wisdom and you get the energy back that you were spending keeping the file closed. Both. Same day.

All of them.

I'll close with the part of my own line that matters most to me, and it's the last four words. With open arms.

Not some lessons. Not the ones I can handle. All of them. Because the moment you start sorting lessons into the ones you'll accept and the ones you won't, you're back to two bins, and you're back to paying. Open arms means the door is open. Whatever's coming, I'll take it, because I already know what it is. It's tuition, and I'm enrolled, and I'm not leaving.

Movement One is done. You've seen that there's no verdict, that the verdict is a tax, and that even your worst chapters were lessons you've been calling by the wrong name. That's the claim of this book, and you've now applied it once to the present and once to the past.

The next movement is harder, because it's about other people. And the sentence that opens it is the one that most people can't hold.

Practice. Name your hardest L. Write it in one line, plainly, the way it actually happened. Underneath it, write the W it gave you, in one sentence. Not what you wish it gave you. What it did. Read the two lines together. That's the true label. Leave it on.
