section 01 · O.M.G. — what it is
Open Minded Goals

A curriculum for the mind that does the learning.

O.M.G. is a cognitive-thinking and rational-behaviour curriculum that runs from Pre-K to 12th grade. It is not a subject and it is not a supplement bolted onto one. It is a parallel spine: a small daily practice that runs alongside everything else the school teaches, and makes everything else the school teaches land better.

It is built as cards, on purpose. Fourteen levels, one per grade band from Pre-K to 12th. Each level has two decks of fifty: a Cognitive Thinking deck and a Rational Thinking deck. One thousand four hundred cards in total, every one written for its age, every one a single thing a young person can do or think through in a few minutes.

Deck What it trains What a card looks like
Cognitive Thinking How the mind processes the world: attention, memory, sequencing, patterns, categories, cause and effect, estimation, reasoning under uncertainty. The machinery of thought. Pre-K: “Matching Shapes — match similar shapes like squares, circles, triangles.” 3rd grade: “Measurement Conversion — convert between units, inches to feet, litres to millilitres.” 12th: “Understanding Advanced Topics in Mathematical Ethics — investigate ethical issues in mathematics, logic and the philosophy of mathematics.”
Rational Thinking How the mind responds to the world: separating a thought from a feeling from an action, weighing choices, noticing distortions, choosing a more open-minded thought, taking responsibility for the response. The character of thought. A situation, a first thought, and a better one — walked through in three steps, then rated: how open was I?
◆ In one sentence

O.M.G. is designed to help you reach your desired outcomes — by training the mind that has to reach them.

The name is the method. Open Minded Goals: a goal is set, the mind is opened to more than its first reaction, and the leader chooses. The word “Open” is doing the work — it is the opposite of the rigid, automatic thinking that cognitive science has identified as the root of most poor decisions at every age.

section 02 · O.M.G. — how it works
The daily hand

Five cards. Two minutes. Every day.

In the Leader portal, O.M.G. is a programme next to reading, maths, science and puzzles. The leader is placed at their level automatically from their grade — a Coach can move them — and chooses a deck. Then they draw.

Step What happens Why it is built this way
1 · Draw a hand Five cards from the leader's level and deck, dealt so no card repeats across recent hands. Small enough to finish. Random enough that the mind cannot coast.
2 · Cognitive cards — do it The leader tries the card for real, with things around them or in their head, then checks in: done, partly, or not yet, and writes one line: what did I notice? The check-in is honest by design. There is no wrong answer to “what did I notice” — only the habit of noticing.
3 · Rational cards — think it through Three lines: the situation · my first thought · a more open-minded thought. Then a 1-to-5: how open was I? This is the cognitive-behavioural move — thought before action — made into a form a nine-year-old can complete and a seventeen-year-old still respects.
4 · Hand summary How many of the five they wrote up, the lines they wrote, XP earned, and a note to their Coach if they want one. Completion is self-reported and never marked right or wrong. What counts is that the thinking happened and was written down.
5 · It lands Every hand writes into Today's Plan, the Progress page, and the Coach's view. Twenty hands complete a level. Nothing is filed away. The Coach sees who is thinking and who is stuck, without standing over anyone.
◆ What “stuck” means here

If a leader completes fewer than three of five cards three hands in a row, the programme flags it to the Coach. Not as a failure — as a signal that the level, the deck, or the day needs a conversation.

section 03 · O.M.G. — how it was designed
The approach — in Jack's words

I did not start with children. I started with the thinking.

O.M.G. grew out of cognitive-thinking work I have been building since 2019: an adult programme, run one-to-one with a life coach, built on a single premise — that most of what goes wrong in a life goes wrong in the thinking first, and that thinking can be trained like anything else. The standards of that programme are still the standards of O.M.G.: openness, honesty, trust, support, and looking honestly at your own role in your own problems instead of blaming them on other people or things.

The science underneath it is not mine and I do not pretend it is. It is the cognitive-behavioural tradition: Aaron Beck's work on cognitive distortions — the automatic, rigid patterns like “should” statements that turn ordinary situations into frustration and resentment — and Albert Ellis's rational-emotive work on replacing rigid demands with flexible preferences. The move is always the same: notice the thought, test it, choose a better one, then act. What I did was ask a question I had not seen answered: what would this look like if you started at four years old instead of forty?

Design principle What it means in practice
Two decks, not one Processing and responding are different skills. A child can be quick and still react badly; a child can be kind and still muddled. Cognitive cards train the machinery. Rational cards train the character. Both, every level, every year.
Fourteen levels, one per year The same idea grows with the child. Pre-K matches shapes and names a feeling; 12th grade weighs the ethics of a mathematical argument. Nobody ages out and nobody is talked down to.
Cards, not chapters A card is one thing. It can be done in minutes, shuffled, drawn again, dealt fresh tomorrow. A chapter cannot. Repetition builds mastery; cards make repetition painless. Today the decks are digital, dealt by the portal on a phone or a laptop; print-ready decks can be produced from the same cards whenever a lab wants them on the table.
No “bad” feelings, no wrong answers From the Pre-K companion guide onward: feelings are named, never labelled bad; a thought can be popped and replaced; a choice is evaluated, not the child. Self-report, not marks, because the honesty is the skill.
Agency is the goal The youngest level ends with “I am the boss of me.” The oldest ends with questions of ethics and responsibility. The whole arc is a child taking ownership of their own mind.
Six focus areas throughout Emotional awareness · rational thinking · leadership mindset · entrepreneurial foundations · self-identity and integrity · cognitive-behaviour tools. Every level carries all six at its own altitude.

section 05 · S.O.S. — what it is
Social Operating Skills

Most children are taught “be yourself.” S.O.S. teaches them to read the room.

S.O.S. is a complete operating system for how a young person moves through other people. Not a worksheet, not a unit: a thesis, four teachable parts, a five-level scale, eighteen named skills, and the activities that practise them. Its core instrument is Social Calibration.

The thesis is the reason it exists. “Be yourself” is the standard advice and it is useless in the moment a child is misreading a room, because it tells them the problem is who they are rather than what they are noticing. S.O.S. replaces identity advice with a skill: adjust the delivery, never the person. A child who cannot read a room is sending one.

The four parts The question the leader learns to ask
01 · Reading the Room Can I notice the emotions, the energy, who is engaged, who feels left out, when tension is rising, when humour lands? Observers before speakers.
02 · Reading Yourself Can I recognise my own emotion, tone, body language, how much space I am taking up, how my actions affect others? Leadership begins with self-awareness.
03 · Choosing an Intentional Response What is needed here? What outcome do I want? What would a great leader do? This is where emotional regulation becomes leadership.
04 · Staying Authentic Am I adjusting my delivery without abandoning my identity? Like the volume on a speaker: the music stays the same, the volume changes with the room.

section 06 · S.O.S. — how it works
The five levels and the eighteen skills

A child is never “bad at people.” They are at a 2 today, and they can name what a 3 looks like.

Level What the leader says about themselves
1 · Unaware “I didn't notice anyone else.”
2 · Noticing “I noticed others, but reacted automatically.”
3 · Adjusting “I recognised what was happening and made a better choice.”
4 · Intentional “I helped improve the group dynamic.”
5 · Leadership “I noticed what others needed before they did, and positively influenced the culture.”

Leaders evaluate themselves after activities against this scale. That single design choice shifts the whole conversation from judgement to growth: there is always a next rung, and the child can say it in their own words. Eighteen skills are practised by name: active listening · eye contact · voice volume · reading facial expressions · personal space · body language · asking questions · timing · humour · conflict resolution · giving feedback · receiving feedback · welcoming new people · including others · navigating disagreements · public speaking · group conversations · one-on-one conversations.

The instruments around it How it works Why it is necessary
I Master Me A named state-shift protocol: Frustrated → Balanced, Overwhelmed → Centered, Anxious → Grounded, Angry → Composed, each with the specific move that gets there. A dysregulated child cannot access a single thing the school teaches. This gives them a tool with a name they can reach for alone.
The 3 B's Brain, Buddy, Book: what do I already know, who around me can help, where is this written down? Three seconds of self-reliance before dependence. It replaces “ask the teacher” as the reflex.
The 10/10 Rule The leader rates their own work, then states what a 10 would have looked like and what would move it there. Self-assessment with teeth. A child who scores their own 6 as a 9 is a coaching signal you would otherwise never see.
The Check-In Ten standing questions per session: proudest moment, the struggle, why, what would help, what changes next time, who they guided and who guided them. Same ten questions every time is what makes eight weeks comparable. That is where the trend lines come from.
360° Feedback Five axes from peers and coaches: Taker↔Giver, Tough-minded↔Warm-hearted, Disagreeable↔Agreeable, Talks-over↔Active-listener, Waits↔Takes-initiative. Character is the one thing a child cannot self-report accurately. The 360 is the only place the group's read enters the record.

section 07 · S.O.S. — how it was designed
The approach — in Jack's words

The volume, not the music.

S.O.S. came from watching what actually separates the young people who thrive from the ones who struggle, and noticing it was almost never intelligence. It was calibration: whether they could feel the room and adjust without losing themselves. Nobody was teaching it, because everyone assumed it could not be taught. So I broke it into parts that can.

Design principle What it means in practice
Skill, not identity The whole system refuses the sentence “that's just how I am.” Reading a room is a skill with parts; parts can be practised; practice can be observed. The child is never the problem — the noticing is.
A scale, not a verdict Five levels with the child's own sentence at each one. Self-placed, not assigned. It gives every leader a next rung instead of a label, and it gives the Coach a common language for the whole lab.
Eighteen names “Good with people” is not teachable. Eye contact, timing, receiving feedback, including others — those are. Naming them is what makes them practisable on a Tuesday and observable by a Coach.
Regulation first I Master Me sits underneath everything, because a child in a flooded state cannot calibrate anything. The tool has a name so they can reach for it alone, and it is logged so regulation becomes visible progress.
The group's read enters the record Self-report is where honesty is trained; the 360 is where reality checks it. Character is the one thing nobody can grade themselves on, so peers and coaches hold that mirror.
Authenticity is a part, not an afterthought Part four exists so that calibration never becomes people-pleasing. The speaker analogy is the whole ethic: change the volume, never the song.

section 09 · Together, and where they live
One system, three surfaces

O.M.G. is the thinking inside a leader. S.O.S. is the thinking between leaders.

They are built to be used together. A Rational Thinking card in O.M.G. asks a leader to choose a more open-minded thought about a situation; S.O.S. is what they use when that situation has other people in it. I Master Me regulates the state; O.M.G. trains the thought; Social Calibration shapes the response. The instruments are shared, the evidence flows into one place, and the Coach sees one child, not three programmes.

The close — nothing to reinvent, nothing to retrain

It drops into the day you already run.

Neither curriculum asks Wonderstorm to change how it teaches. They slot into the rhythm the school already has — Morning Circle, Core Academics, Expedition Time, Closing Circle — and into the portal the leaders already open. Here is what integration actually means, for the student and for the teacher, starting the day you say go.

For the student For the teacher
O.M.G. Opens the portal at Morning Circle, draws today's five cards at their own level, writes a line per card. Two minutes. It sits beside their daily goals — same screen, same ritual. Nothing to prepare. The portal deals the cards, at each leader's level, and keeps the record. The Coach view shows who is thinking and who is stuck. A Coach can move a leader up or down a level with one click.
S.O.S. After an activity they were already going to do — a discussion, a group build, an expedition — they place themselves on the five-level scale in one tap and name the skill they practised. Thirty seconds. Nothing to teach that you do not already teach; the scale gives the conversation you already have a common language. Levels, 360s and check-ins land in the same Coach view. I Master Me is a name a child can reach for alone.
Lesson planning Unchanged. The cards and the scale live around the lessons, not inside them. Unchanged. No new units, no new worksheets, no new curriculum map. The community agreements are edited in the portal, by you, whenever you like.
Training None. A leader who can open the portal can draw a hand. One twenty-minute walkthrough of the Coach view. Everything else is the school's own words and the school's own rhythm.
Day one Tuesday morning, when the leaders return: log in, draw, write, go. Tuesday morning: open the Coach view at Closing Circle and read what the lab thought about today.
